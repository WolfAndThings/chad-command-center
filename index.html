Mon Feb 10 Story ~190w
The controller who waited 3 years
Manufacturing company outside Detroit. Controller who's been in the role 11 years. We're on a call last quarter and she says something that just stops me.
Read Full Post →
Manufacturing company outside Detroit. Controller who's been in the role 11 years. We're on a call last quarter and she says something that just stops me. "I've known we needed to change for three years. I just couldn't get anyone to care enough to do something about it." Three years. She knew. Her team knew. The data was sitting right there. But nobody above her felt enough pain to move. And I realized sitting there that my job isn't to convince anyone of anything. It's to find the people who already know and help them build the internal momentum they can't build alone. That's a completely different thing than selling. She signed 4 months later. $187K. Not because I convinced her. She was convinced before I ever showed up. I just gave her the language and the data to convince everyone else. I think about her a lot. How many controllers are sitting in that exact position right now. Knowing. Waiting. Frustrated. Three years is a long time to know something and not be able to do anything about it.
Tue Feb 11 Personal ~210w
I got fired once
I got fired once. I was 26. I'd been at the company maybe a year. I was loud. I was confident. I thought showing up and being smart was the whole job.
Read Full Post →
I got fired once. I was 26. I'd been at the company maybe a year. I was loud. I was confident. I thought showing up and being smart was the whole job. It wasn't. My manager pulled me into a room on a Tuesday afternoon. Two HR people I'd never met. One of them had a folder. That's when I knew. I don't remember the drive home. I remember sitting in my apartment staring at the wall. Rent due in 9 days. $1,200 in my checking account. The thing about getting fired is it strips away every story you've been telling yourself. Every "I'm talented enough to figure it out" and "the rules don't apply to me." They do apply to you. Talent without discipline is just wasted potential with a good personality. Those 3 months of rebuilding built the foundation for everything after. The 179% average, the #1 ranking, the Platinum Club years. All of it started in that apartment staring at a wall. I don't tell this story to be inspirational. I tell it because I think there's someone reading this going through something similar right now. And the worst moment of my career was also the most important one. Not because everything happens for a reason. That's lazy thinking. Because getting knocked down forced me to build something I never would have built while I was coasting.
Wed Feb 12 Sales ~175w
22 minutes of silence
Asked a buyer yesterday what happens if they don't do anything. If they just keep running things the way they're running them.
Read Full Post →
Asked a buyer yesterday what happens if they don't do anything. If they just keep running things the way they're running them. She went quiet. Not the polite "let me think about it" quiet. The kind where you can tell someone is seeing something they haven't let themselves see before. "Honestly? I think I'd lose my best analyst. She's been telling me for 6 months that she can't keep doing this manually. And I keep telling her we're working on it." That's it. That's the whole post. A buyer connecting their inaction to a real person on their team. That's where everything changes. Not your deck. Not your pricing. I didn't say anything after that. Fifteen seconds of silence, maybe twenty. Felt like five minutes. Then she said "OK. What does the timeline look like to get this done before Q3?" I didn't close that deal. She closed it herself. I just created the space for her to see what she already knew. Sometimes the most important thing you can do on a call is absolutely nothing.
Thu Feb 13 Personal ~170w
My dad's advice
My dad's not in sales. He's an engineer, thinks in systems, doesn't say much.
Read Full Post →
My dad's not in sales. He's an engineer. Thinks in systems. Doesn't say much. When I got my first sales job he told me "the people who are best at their jobs are the ones who do the parts nobody sees." I didn't understand it at the time. I thought sales was the pitch, the demo, the close. The stuff people see. It's not. It's the 5 minutes of research before a call that nobody will ever know about. It's updating your CRM at 6pm on a Friday when nobody's watching. It's writing a thoughtful follow-up that takes 20 minutes instead of a "just checking in" that takes 20 seconds. My dad was right. He usually is. But I had to figure it out the hard way because I was 24 and thought I knew everything. 239% quota doesn't happen in the meeting room. It happens at 6pm on a Friday when you're updating notes because you know Future You will need them. Nobody claps for that. Nobody sees it. That's the point.
Fri Feb 14 Story ~200w
The deal I broke with one sentence
2022. VP of Finance and I had been working together 6 weeks. She told me things about her org she probably shouldn't have. That's how much she trusted me.
Read Full Post →
2022. VP of Finance and I had been working together 6 weeks. Good relationship. Honest conversations. She told me things about her org she probably shouldn't have. That's how much she trusted me. Then I screwed up. Meeting with her boss, the CFO. I casually mentioned something she'd told me in confidence. Wasn't trying to go around her. Just wasn't thinking. She called me after. Voice flat. Not angry. Worse. "I told you that because I trusted you. Not so you could use it to look smart in front of my boss." Deal still closed. Relationship never fully recovered. She was cordial, professional, but the openness was gone. The real conversations stopped. I became just another vendor. I think about that phone call probably once a month. Not because I lost a relationship, although I did. Because it taught me that trust isn't a sales technique. It's a responsibility. And the moment you break it, even accidentally, even with good intentions, it's over. I never made that mistake again. But I had to make it once to understand what trust actually costs when you break it. Happy Valentine's Day. Go protect someone's trust today instead of trying to earn more of it.

Tue Feb 18 Sales ~195w
Interested, not interesting
Had a conversation with a rep here yesterday that I can't stop thinking about. She's been at Ramp maybe 8 months. Crushing it. Top of the board.
Read Full Post →
Had a conversation with a rep here yesterday that I can't stop thinking about. She's been at Ramp maybe 8 months. Crushing it. Top of the board. I asked her what she thinks makes the difference. Expecting her to say something about the product or the market or the leads. She said "I stopped trying to be interesting and started trying to be interested." Then she told me about a call she'd had that morning. CFO of a logistics company. Instead of pitching she spent the first 15 minutes asking about his month-end close process. Not to find pain points. Not to build a business case. Just to understand. He ended up talking for 22 minutes straight. She barely said a word. At the end he said "I've never had a sales rep actually understand what my days look like. When can we talk again?" That's it. That's the whole strategy. Not a framework. Not a methodology. Just a person who genuinely wanted to understand another person's world. 15 years doing this and I'm still learning from people who've been doing it 8 months. That's either humbling or exciting. Probably both.
Wed Feb 19 Sales ~200w
The meeting after the meeting
You crush a demo. Everyone's nodding. Your champion is fired up. "This is exactly what we need." "I'm going to talk to the team."
Read Full Post →
You crush a demo. Everyone's nodding. Your champion is fired up. "This is exactly what we need." "I'm going to talk to the team." You hang up feeling great. Update your CRM. Tell your manager the deal is moving. Two weeks of silence. What happened is your champion tried to sell it internally and got destroyed. Walked into a meeting with their CFO who said "we have 14 other priorities, why should I care about this one?" And your champion didn't have an answer. Because you never gave them one. I lost a $340K deal this way in 2021. Everything was perfect on my side of the table. I just forgot that the real sale was happening on the other side. In rooms I'd never be in. In conversations I'd never hear. Now I spend as much time preparing my champion for their internal meetings as I do preparing for my own. Sometimes more. "Here's what your CFO is going to ask. Here's the data that answers it." You're not selling at that point. You're coaching. And that's the difference between deals that stall and deals that close.
Thu Feb 20 Reflection ~175w
The years I forgot about quota
The best year of my career, 239%, number 1 out of 75. I never once thought about my quota during a call.
Read Full Post →
The best year of my career. 239%. Number 1 out of 75. I never once thought about my quota during a call. Not once. I thought about the person on the other end. What their day looks like. Whether I could actually help them or whether I was wasting both our time. The year I almost didn't make club was 2019. Hit 103%. I thought about quota constantly. Every call, in the back of my head, this running calculation. "If this one closes I'm at 87%. If I pull in the deal from March I'm at 94%." And buyers could feel it. I know they could. Because the quality of my conversations dropped. I stopped asking hard questions because I was afraid the answers would kill the deal. Started discounting early because I wanted the signature more than I wanted the right deal. I don't have some profound solution for this. I don't have a trick to stop thinking about it. I just know that the years I forgot about quota were the years I crushed it. And the years I obsessed over it were the years I barely made it. Make of that what you will.

Mon Feb 24 Sales ~210w
Commission breath
"Hey, I've got a couple kids. Do I work on commission? Yeah. Would I love to sell you as much stuff as possible? That'd be great. But that stuff's not important to you."
Read Full Post →
"Hey, I've got a couple kids. Do I work on commission? Yeah. Would I love to sell you as much stuff as possible? That'd be great. But that stuff's not important to you." I say that on my first call with every buyer. Sales leaders hate it. First time I said it I was 28 and nervous and it just fell out of my mouth. Wasn't a strategy. Was more of a panic response honestly. The buyer laughed, whole energy shifted, and we had a real conversation instead of whatever that other thing is where two adults pretend one of them isn't trying to sell something. I call it commission breath. You know what I'm talking about. When a rep needs the deal so bad the buyer can smell it. Every question has a pitch hiding behind it. The whole thing feels like a first date where one person already picked out wedding venues. Naming it out loud puts the elephant on the table. "Yeah, I want to sell you stuff. Now that we've said that out loud, can we actually talk?" Buyers have told me months later that the reason they picked me over the other vendor was that moment. Not my demo. Not my pricing. The fact that I said the quiet part loud.

Wed Feb 26 Reflection ~200w
Is honesty still honest when it's intentional?
Every first call, I say some version of: "I work on commission. I've got two kids. I would love to sell you everything in our catalog. But that's not what this conversation is about."
Read Full Post →
Every first call, I say some version of: "I work on commission. I've got two kids. I would love to sell you everything in our catalog. But that's not what this conversation is about." It always works. People laugh, they relax, the call goes better. But lately I've been wondering. Is that honesty or is it a move? Am I being genuine or have I just learned that performing vulnerability works? I don't have a clean answer. I think it started genuine. First time I said it, years ago, it just came out. Buyer laughed. Call went great. I thought "huh, that worked." And then I kept saying it. Does something stop being honest when it becomes intentional? When you know the effect it'll have? I don't think so. Because the underlying truth hasn't changed. I do work on commission. I do have two kids. I genuinely don't want to waste their time. The difference is between manipulation and transparency. Manipulation is saying something false to get a reaction. Transparency is saying something true that also happens to build trust. Both get results. Only one you can live with. Still working this one out.
Thu Feb 27 Ramp ~175w
Curiosity vs. methodology
Week 2 at Ramp. Something clicked today. Not about the product. About me.
Read Full Post →
Week 2 at Ramp. Something clicked today. Not about the product. About me. I was shadowing a call with one of the senior reps. She was talking to a CFO at a logistics company. And I noticed something in how she asked questions. She wasn't following a framework. Wasn't running through a discovery checklist. She was having a conversation. A real one. Where one question flowed naturally from the answer to the last one. And I realized I used to do that. Years ago. Before I got good enough to have a "process." Somewhere along the way I replaced curiosity with methodology. Turned genuine interest into a repeatable system. 179% proves the system works. But watching her today reminded me of something I'd forgotten. The system is supposed to serve the conversation. Not the other way around. Starting at a new company strips away the muscle memory. Forces you to be present again because you don't have a system yet. You're just you. Trying to understand. That might be more powerful than any framework. I'm going to sit with that for a while.

Last day of February. Four weeks ago I was wrapping up at my old company. Saying goodbye to people I'd worked with for years. Feeling a mix of excitement and grief I wasn't expecting.
