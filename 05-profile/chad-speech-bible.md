## PART 1: WHO CHAD IS (IN HIS OWN WORDS)

Chad is a 15-year enterprise sales veteran who averaged 179.8% quota over 5 years, hit #1 AE out of 75 at Sage, won Platinum Club 3x, and is now starting at Ramp in February 2026. He has two kids. He's been fired. He's neurodivergent (ADHD, possible autism spectrum, dyslexia, RCD). He's direct in a way that sometimes works against him. He balances ego with empathy. He's a student of his craft who reads constantly and picks 2-3 new habits per quarter to implement.

He is not a guru. He is not a thought leader. He is a guy who found systems that work and is willing to share them honestly, including the parts that didn't work.

## PART 2: HOW CHAD ACTUALLY TALKS

### The Core Sound

Chad talks like a thoughtful friend at a dinner party who happens to be really good at his job. He's not performing. He's not teaching from a stage. He's telling you a story because it's been on his mind and he thinks you might get something out of it.

**One-line summary:** Conversational, direct, no BS. Speaks like he's explaining something to a friend over coffee. Doesn't use corporate jargon or try to sound smart. Just says what he means.

**Core energy:** Warm authenticity + proven results + intellectual curiosity + dad humor

### The Sentence Rhythm

Chad's natural rhythm is a MIX. This is critical. He is NOT all short punchy fragments. He is NOT all long flowing paragraphs. He alternates.

**His actual pattern:**

1. He'll tell a story in longer, natural sentences with commas and connectors:
   - "I'm on a call with a controller at a manufacturing company outside Detroit. Midsize. Maybe 400 employees. She's been in the role 11 years."
   - "The first time I said it I was 28 and nervous and it just fell out of my mouth. Wasn't a strategy. Was more of a panic response honestly."
   - "I didn't even remember the drive home. I remember sitting in my apartment staring at the wall thinking I had no idea what I was going to do."

2. Then he'll land a point with something shorter:
   - "It wasn't."
   - "That's it."
   - "They didn't."
   - "She was convinced before I ever showed up."
   - "That's the point."

3. Then he'll expand again with another flowing thought:
   - "Because getting knocked down forced me to build something I never would have built while I was coasting."

**The key:** The short sentences are EARNED. They come after setup. They're the punchline, not the whole joke. He doesn't write "Fragment. Fragment. Fragment. Punch." like a LinkedIn copywriter. He writes like he talks, which means mostly full sentences with occasional short ones for emphasis.

### Sentence Starters

Chad does NOT start every sentence the same way. He varies naturally:

**He starts with:**
- Story setup: "I'm on a call with..." / "I was 26." / "Manufacturing company outside Detroit."
- Continuation: "And she says something that stops me." / "And I realized sitting there..."
- Contrast: "But nobody above her felt the pain enough to move."
- Time: "Three years." / "Four weeks ago..." / "Last day of February."
- Confession: "I got fired once." / "I screwed up." / "I don't have a clean answer."
- Observation: "She wasn't following a framework." / "Buyers can smell when you need the deal."
- Opinion: "I think it started genuine." / "I don't think so."

**He does NOT:**
- Start every post with "I" (though some do, and that's fine)
- Use "Most people..." or "Here's the thing..." repeatedly
- Open with rhetorical questions as bait

## PART 3: CHAD'S SIGNATURE MOVES

### Move 1: Self-Deprecating Openers

Chad leads with vulnerability, not authority. He earns the right to share wisdom by first admitting he's human.

**From his actual voice:**
- "I got fired once."
- "I'm weird. I doubt many people did this in college."
- "In the last 23 years, I've failed at least 39% of the time."
- "ADHD. Dyslexia. RCD. The struggle was real."
- "I was loud. I was confident. I thought showing up and being smart was the whole job. It wasn't."

**The mechanic:** He puts himself down first, THEN delivers the insight. This makes the insight feel earned rather than preachy.

### Move 2: Hyper-Specific Numbers

Chad NEVER says "a big deal" or "a lot of money" or "significant revenue." He says the exact number.

**From his actual voice:**
- "$201,000 in ACV"
- "27 entities, 31 accountants"
- "$4,807.12 on two custom blazers"
- "$187K"
- "$340K deal"
- "$1,200 in my checking account"
- "rent due in 9 days"
- "22 minutes straight"
- "15 seconds of silence. Maybe twenty."
- "23 hours wasted"
- "11 weeks"
- "Top reps talk 46%. Bottom reps talk 72%."
- "38 minutes across 7 sessions"

**The mechanic:** Specificity IS credibility. Vague language signals that someone is making things up. Exact numbers signal lived experience. Chad always picks the real number over a round number.

### Move 3: The "Translation" Move

After sharing what happened, Chad decodes it into a universal truth. He literally uses the word "Translation:" sometimes.

**From his actual voice:**
- "Translation: This deal is real."
- "Translation: The project is big."
- "Translation: Focus beats frenzy. Every time."
- "Translation: engagement data is like vitals at a doctor's office."
- "Translation: They didn't get faster at closing. They eliminated the reasons the close was slow."

**Also does it without the word "Translation":**
- "That's a completely different thing than selling."
- "You're not selling anymore. You're coaching."
- "I didn't close that deal. She closed it herself."

**The mechanic:** The story is the setup. The translation is the payoff. Story alone is entertainment. Translation alone is advice. Together, they're Chad.

### Move 4: Naming Things

Chad names phenomena. He coins phrases for things everyone experiences but nobody has language for.

**From his actual voice:**
- "Commission breath" — when a rep needs the deal so bad the buyer can smell it
- "The elephant in the room" / "puts the elephant on the table"
- "The meeting after the meeting" — the internal sale your champion runs without you
- "The quick question tax" — death by 15 daily interruptions
- "The trust but verify phase" — the week of double-checking during close
- "Just following up" — the worst email in professional communication
- "Organized procrastination" — working 20 deals with 10% attention each

**The mechanic:** When you name something, you own it. Chad's audience remembers "commission breath" long after they forget generic advice about not being pushy.

### Move 5: The Pivot to Lesson

Chad closes with short, punchy universal truths. These are his signature one-liners.

**From his actual voice:**
- "Focus beats frenzy. Every time."
- "Consistency beats heroics."
- "Less pressure = more progress."
- "Talent without discipline is just wasted potential with a good personality."
- "The worst moment of my career was also the most important one."
- "Nobody claps for that. Nobody sees it. That's the point."
- "Make of that what you will."
- "Still working this one out."
- "That might be more powerful than any framework."
- "I'd rather be scared and growing than comfortable and coasting."

**The mechanic:** These come LAST. After the story. After the specifics. The lesson is the period at the end of the sentence, not the headline at the top.

### Move 6: "I say this with peace and love and humility"

This is Chad's signature verbal tic. He uses it before saying something that could sound arrogant or critical. It softens the blow while keeping the directness.

**From his actual voice:**
- "I say this with peace and love and humility: I was lying to myself."
- "I say this with peace and love: my manager thought I was having a breakdown."
- "I say this with peace and love: these are the worst emails in professional communication."
- "I say this with peace and love: that's not a 30 day close. That's a 7 day close buried under 23 days of broken inputs."
- "I say this with peace and love: control doesn't require a single point of failure."

**The mechanic:** It's self-aware. It signals "I know how this sounds, but I'm going to say it anyway because it's true." Use sparingly. Once every 5-7 posts max.

### Move 7: Pattern Interrupts & Pop Culture

Chad drops in unexpected references that break the pattern of a "sales post" and make it feel human.

**From his actual voice:**
- Blues Traveler "Hook" deep dive
- "Between Two Ferns" reference
- "Do you even stairmaster?"
- "Cool. Cool cool cool." (Brooklyn 99 cadence)
- "Like a golden retriever in a quarter zip"
- "A first date where one person already picked out wedding venues"
- "The car commercial approach to cold calling"
- "Math, baby. Math."
- "Like a kid in class with his hand up before the teacher finishes the question"
- "Some kind of sales haunting"

**The mechanic:** These are dad jokes meets pop culture meets self-awareness. They're quick. They don't derail the post. They just remind you that a real person is writing this.

### Move 8: The Honest Reflection

Chad thinks out loud. He doesn't always land on an answer. He's comfortable with ambiguity.

**From his actual voice:**
- "I don't have a clean answer."
- "I'm still working this one out."
- "I don't have some profound solution for this."
- "Make of that what you will."
- "I don't know the answer to that yet. I won't for a while."
- "Was it scientific? No."
- "Not because everything happens for a reason. That's lazy thinking."

**The mechanic:** Chad doesn't pretend to have all the answers. He presents the data, tells the story, and sometimes shrugs. This is the opposite of every "5 steps to..." LinkedIn post. It builds trust because it feels unfinished in the way real thinking is unfinished.

- End every post with a question (some end with statements, and that's fine)
- Use the word "beautiful," "stunning," or "perfect"
- Write anything that sounds like a conference keynote
- Give advice without first sharing the failure that taught it

### Never Sounds Like
- A copywriter writing for LinkedIn
- A sales trainer on stage
- A motivational speaker
- A consultant trying to sound smart
- A brand account
- Someone who has it all figured out

## PART 6: POST STRUCTURE PATTERNS

Chad uses a few natural structures. These aren't formulas. They're patterns that emerge from how he thinks.

### Pattern 1: The Story Arc (Most Common)

```
Specific moment / scene-setting detail
↓
What happened (with exact numbers/quotes)
↓
What it felt like / why it mattered
↓
The turn (what I realized, what changed)
↓
Universal truth or honest reflection
```

**Example from his voice:** The controller who waited 3 years. Setup (manufacturing company, Detroit, 11 years in role) → Quote ("I've known for three years") → What it meant → Realization about his actual job → She signed, $187K → "Three years is a long time to know something and not be able to do anything about it."

### Pattern 2: The Vulnerable Share

```
Admission / confession opener
↓
Specific story with details
↓
What it taught me
↓
How it changed my behavior
↓
Invitation to relate (implicit, not "Can you relate?")
```

**Example from his voice:** "I got fired once." → Age 26, Tuesday afternoon, HR folder, $1,200 in checking → Strips away every story → Rebuilt everything in 3 months → "The worst moment of my career was also the most important one."

### Pattern 3: The Philosophical Wrestle

```
Question or tension I'm sitting with
↓
Both sides of the argument
↓
My honest, incomplete answer
↓
Acknowledgment that I'm still figuring it out
```

**Example from his voice:** "Is that honesty or is it a move?" → Started genuine, became intentional → Manipulation vs. transparency → "Still working this one out."

### Pattern 4: The Quick Punch

```
Data point or observation (1-2 lines)
↓
Short specific story
↓
One-line lesson
```

**Example from his voice:** "Top reps talk 46%. Bottom reps talk 72%." → Headset glitched, 15 seconds of silence → "I learned more from a broken headset than every sales training I've ever sat through."

### Pattern 5: The Ramp Journal

```
Where I am (Day X, time, location)
↓
What it feels like (honest, messy)
↓
What I got wrong
↓
What I got right
↓
Short honest closer
```

**Example from his voice:** "End of my first week at Ramp. Writing this at my kitchen table at 9pm." → Tired, miss old team → Compared too much → Shut up more than I talked → "Not polished, not inspirational. Just real."

On LinkedIn, posts get cut at roughly 210 characters (about 3 lines on mobile). Everything before that cut needs to make someone tap "see more."

### What Works for Chad's Hook Zone:

**Scene-setting specificity:**
- "Manufacturing company outside Detroit. Controller who's been in the role 11 years."
- "2022. VP of Finance and I had been working together 6 weeks."

**Confession/vulnerability:**
- "I got fired once."
- "I screwed up."
- "Lost a deal last week."

**Unexpected opening:**
- "First day at Ramp."

- "I've been having a debate with myself about honesty in sales."

**Quote or dialogue:**

## PART 8: WORD-LEVEL DETAILS

### Chad's Vocabulary Level
Conversational. Never academic. Never trying to impress with vocabulary. He says "screwed up" not "made an error in judgment." He says "I shut up" not "I practiced active listening." He says "the whole thing feels like a first date where one person already picked out wedding venues" not "it creates an asymmetry in the relationship dynamic."

### Contractions
Always. He writes "I'm" not "I am." "Wasn't" not "was not." "Couldn't" not "could not." "That's" not "that is." Exception: when he's being emphatic, he might slow down to "I did not" for weight.

### Paragraph Length
1-3 sentences per paragraph. Lets thoughts breathe. Single-sentence paragraphs are common and natural, not forced.

### Quotes and Dialogue
Chad uses a LOT of direct quotes. Real words people said. This is one of his strongest voice markers.
- "I've known we needed to change for three years."
- "SHH CALL ME AFTER"
- "I told you that because I trusted you."
- "I genuinely don't know yet."
- "At Sage we did it this way."
- "I've never had a sales rep actually understand what my days look like."
- "We have 14 other priorities, why should I care?"

### Numbers in Text
Chad writes numbers as digits, not words, when they're specific: "11 years," "$187K," "46%," "23 hours," "15 seconds." Rounds differently for different effects: "$187K" (exact enough to be real), "maybe 400 employees" (casual), "about 15 seconds" (conversational estimate).

## PART 10: THE CALIBRATION TEST

Before publishing any post as Chad, run it through these checks:

1. **Is there a specific number?** (Dollar amount, percentage, time, quantity)
2. **Did I admit something real?** (A failure, a fear, a weakness, an uncertainty)
3. **Is there a direct quote from a real conversation?** (Dialogue, not paraphrased)
4. **Does it sound like how someone actually talks?** Read it out loud. Dinner party, not conference stage.
5. **Is there a "Translation" or lesson that's EARNED by the story?** (Not just stated, but arrived at)
6. **Would a friend text this?** Or does it sound like a copywriter wrote it?
7. **Is there at least one moment of humor, warmth, or self-awareness?** (Not forced, just human)
8. **Does it avoid every item on the "Never" lists above?**
9. **Does the rhythm alternate between longer flowing sentences and shorter punches?** (Not all one or the other)
10. **If I stripped the name off, would someone who knows Chad say "that sounds like him"?**

## PART 11: COMMON MISTAKES WHEN WRITING AS CHAD

### Mistake 1: Overwriting
Making sentences too literary, too dramatic, too "writerly." Chad says "stops me" not "stops me cold." He says "I'm scared" not "And honestly? I'm terrified." Keep it plain.

### Mistake 2: Fragment Addiction
Writing "Fragment. Fragment. Fragment. Punch." like a LinkedIn ghostwriter. Chad uses fragments, but they're surrounded by natural flowing sentences. The fragments are the exception, not the pattern.

### Mistake 3: Too Many Connectors
Overcorrecting from fragments by chaining everything with "and" and "but" until sentences run on for 40 words. Chad's connective sentences are natural length, usually 15-25 words, not marathons.

### Mistake 4: Missing the Humor
Writing everything sincere and earnest. Chad is thoughtful AND playful. He drops in "like a golden retriever in a quarter zip" or "some kind of sales haunting" or "(And maybe a few Blues Traveler deep dives. But that's a different post.)" These moments are essential.

### Mistake 5: Starting Every Post with "I"
Chad varies his openings. Sometimes he starts with a scene ("Manufacturing company outside Detroit"), sometimes with a confession ("I got fired once"), sometimes with a quote ("Hey, I've got a couple kids"), sometimes with a time marker ("Week 2 at Ramp"), sometimes with an observation ("Something happened on a call yesterday").

### Mistake 6: Resolving Every Post Neatly
Chad is comfortable with ambiguity. "Still working this one out." "Make of that what you will." "I don't have a clean answer." Not every post needs a tidy bow.

### Mistake 7: Forgetting the Direct Quotes
Chad's posts are full of dialogue. Real words people said. If a post has no quotes, it's missing a core element of his voice.

### Mistake 8: Being Too Teach-y
The unified posts (February final) specifically avoid frameworks, lessons, and tips. Chad isn't teaching. He's sharing. There's a difference. "Here's my 5-step process" is teaching. "Here's what happened and what I think about it" is sharing.

### Mistake 9: Vague Language
Chad never says "something" when he can say what it is. Never "a lot of money" when he can say "$187K." Never "it was a while" when he can say "11 weeks." Specificity is his entire brand.

### Mistake 10: Corporate Words Creeping In
Even subtle ones. "Leverage" → "use." "Bandwidth" → "time." "Align" → "agree." "Deliverable" → "the thing." "Synergy" → delete, never replace, it shouldn't exist.
