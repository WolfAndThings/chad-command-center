### Mon Feb 10 — 23 deals

I had 23 deals in my pipeline once.

My manager loved me that month. Look at that forecast. Look at all those opportunities. Chad's cooking.

I closed two of them.

I say this with peace and love and humility: I was lying to myself. I knew at least 12 of those were dead. The contacts had gone quiet. Timelines slipped. Budgets were "still being discussed," which is corporate for "we're not doing this."

But I kept them in because deleting a deal from your pipeline feels like admitting you failed. And I didn't want to admit that on a Tuesday.

Here's what 23 deals actually looked like:

23 deals x 10% of my attention = maybe 2 deals worth of actual effort.

Every follow up a little too generic. Every call a little too surface. I couldn't remember the details because there were too many details to remember.

I work 3-5 deals now. That's it.

First quarter I did it, hit 147%. Next quarter, 163%.

I didn't get smarter. I got honest about what deserved my time.

Translation: Focus beats frenzy. Every time.

### Tue Feb 11 — Commission breath

I say something on my first call with every buyer that makes sales leaders uncomfortable.

"Hey, I've got a couple kids. Do I work on commission? Yeah. Would I love to sell you as much stuff as possible? That'd be great. But that stuff's not important to you."

The first time I said it I was 28 and nervous and it just fell out of my mouth. Wasn't a strategy. Was more of a panic response honestly.

The buyer laughed. Whole energy shifted. We had a real conversation instead of whatever that other thing is where two adults pretend one of them isn't trying to sell something.

I call it commission breath. You know what I'm talking about. When a rep needs the deal so bad the buyer can smell it. Every question has a pitch hiding behind it. Every "that's really interesting" is just waiting for an opening. The whole thing feels like a first date where one person already picked out wedding venues.

Buyers know. They always know.

Naming it out loud just puts the elephant on the table. "Yeah, I want to sell you stuff. Now that we've said that out loud, can we actually talk?"

I've had buyers tell me MONTHS later that the reason they picked me over the other vendor was that moment. Not my demo. Not my pricing.

The fact that I said the quiet part loud.

### Wed Feb 12 — The headset glitch

Quick one today.

Top reps talk 46% of the time on calls. Bottom reps talk 72%.

I used to be a 72 percenter. Thought my job was convincing people. If I could just explain it well enough, clearly enough, with enough slides, they'd get it.

They didn't.

Then my headset glitched on a call with a VP of Operations outside Nashville. Muted me for about 15 seconds mid-question.

In those 15 seconds she told me more about her actual problem than she had in the previous 20 minutes.

Because nobody was interrupting her.

I learned more from a broken headset than every sales training I've ever sat through.

Now I count to 5 after every question before I say anything. Even when it's awkward. ESPECIALLY when it's awkward.

Do you even stairmaster? Just kidding. But seriously. The silence thing. Try it tomorrow.

### Thu Feb 13 — 11 weeks I'll never get back

Manufacturing company in Ohio. Director of Ops. Great guy. Took every call. Asked smart questions. Seemed genuinely engaged.

Couldn't get me to the CFO.

"She's traveling."
"It's quarter close."
"Let me get a few more details first."

11 weeks of this. ELEVEN. I built a business case. Customized a demo. Brought in a solutions engineer. The whole production.

CFO never joined a single call.

Deal died. Not because the director didn't want it. Because he didn't have the authority to make it happen and I let him convince me he eventually would.

23 hours. I calculated it later. 23 hours on a deal that was never real.

That's 3 full days of selling I gave to a ghost.

Now when I can't get to the person who writes the check after 2 real asks, I have an honest conversation. "Hey, I want to be respectful of your time and mine. Is there something blocking this from getting to your CFO? Because if the timing isn't right, that's totally fine. I'd rather know now."

People respect that. Even when the answer is bad news.

### Fri Feb 14 — The year I stopped selling (Happy Valentine's Day)

The year I hit 239% I spent the first 6 weeks not selling.

I say this with peace and love: my manager thought I was having a breakdown.

"Your activity numbers are low. What's going on?"

I was sending articles to prospects I hadn't talked to in months. Not our articles. Just stuff I thought they'd find useful. Industry reports. Conference recaps. A note that said "saw this and thought of you."

No ask. No calendar link. No "when can we reconnect." Just... value.

By week 7, three of those people called ME. Unprompted. One said "I've been meaning to reach out, we're finally ready." Another forwarded my email to her CFO with "this is the rep I was telling you about."

By Q2 my pipeline was closing itself.

Not because I got better at pitching. Because when those buyers had a need, they thought of the guy who showed up when he didn't need anything.

Happy Valentine's Day. Go send someone something useful with zero strings attached. See what happens in 6 months.

### Tue Feb 18 — Account A vs. Account B

After a demo I always know if they're actually looking at what I sent them.

Not because I'm psychic. Because I track it.

I use digital sales rooms. First week I had tracking on, I had two accounts at similar stages. Both had great calls. Both champions said all the right stuff. "This is exactly what we need." "I'm going to share this with the team."

You know the script.

Account A: 38 minutes across 7 sessions. Looked at pricing. Forwarded the room to 2 teammates. Went deep on implementation timelines.

Account B: hadn't logged in once.

Guess which one closed?

If I wasn't tracking, I would've treated them identically. Same follow up. Same energy. Same confidence in my forecast.

Translation: engagement data is like vitals at a doctor's office. It tells you what's actually happening, not what the patient says is happening.

The buyer who spent 38 minutes is building their internal case. They're getting ready.

The buyer who said "I'll review it" and never opened it? No amount of "just circling back" is gonna fix that.

### Wed Feb 19 — What are your three questions?

I do something in the first 60 seconds of every first call that I've literally never seen another rep do.

Before any agenda slide. Before "tell me about your role." Before any of the standard stuff.

I say: "This call is not about me. This call is about you. My agenda is not important. If we walked away today and I didn't answer the three questions you came with, it'd be a total waste of your time. So. What are those three questions?"

Then I shut up.

There's always a pause. Because nobody has ever asked them that. They're used to 15 minutes of slides before anyone asks what they actually care about.

Had a CFO once whose three questions were:

"Can this integrate with our ERP without blowing up month end?"
"What does the first 90 days actually look like, not the sales version?"
"Are your other finance customers actually happy or did you cherry pick the case studies?"

That's a roadmap. In 90 seconds. She just told me exactly what she needs, what she's skeptical about, and where to build trust.

If I'd started with my deck I would've spent 15 minutes on slides that didn't address any of that. She would've checked out by slide 4.

I do this every single first call now.

### Thu Feb 20 — Just following up

I have a confession.

I used to send "just following up" emails. Hundreds of them probably.

"Just circling back."
"Just touching base."
"Just wanted to check in."

I say this with peace and love: these are the worst emails in professional communication. And I sent SO MANY of them.

Here's what "just following up" actually communicates: "I have nothing new for you. No new insight. No new data. No new reason you should care. But your name is on my list and I need to hit my activity number."

Nobody has ever read "just following up" and thought "wow, I'm glad they sent that."

I stopped cold turkey. If I don't have something new to say, the email doesn't get sent. Period.

What I do instead: "I was looking at your 10-K and noticed your SG&A climbing about 3% year over year. Put together a quick model showing where automation hits that hardest. Worth 10 minutes Thursday?"

That email takes 15 minutes to write. The "just following up" takes 30 seconds.

My response rate on the 15-minute version: ~60%.
The 30-second version: ~8%.

Math, baby. Math.

### Fri Feb 21 — Lottery test

Someone asked me what I'd do differently if I won the lottery tomorrow.

And I realized: I'd sell exactly the same way.

Not a humble brag. (OK maybe a small one.) But it's real.

When I don't need a deal, I ask harder questions. I disqualify faster. I push back on bad timelines. I tell people when a competitor might actually be a better fit.

And here's the weird part: every time I walk away from a deal, two more show up. Because buyers talk to each other. And apparently "the sales rep who told me I wasn't ready yet" is a story people tell their friends.

My best year ever (239%) I had the deepest pipeline of my career. Not because I was working more deals. Because I wasn't gripping any of them.

Sell like you're independently wealthy.

Not because you don't care. Because you care about the right things.

### Mon Feb 24 — Nice guy, no quota

There's a rep I used to work with. Everyone loved this guy. Hilarious. Warm. Remembered your kid's name, your dog's birthday, your go-to coffee order. Like a golden retriever in a quarter zip.

Missed quota 6 quarters in a row.

Because he confused being liked with being trusted.

Small talk is "How was your weekend?" It makes YOU comfortable. It fills the silence. It builds a connection that feels real but isn't.

I don't do a ton of small talk. I open with stuff like "What made you take this call today?"

That question skips past the pleasantries and says: I respect your time. Be direct with me.

My friend kept getting calls because buyers genuinely enjoyed talking to him. But they signed with the rep who challenged them. The one who said "I don't think that timeline is realistic and here's why."

The one they trusted.

He's in marketing now. Still a great dude. Just wasn't built for the part of sales that requires making people uncomfortable for 30 seconds in order to save them 6 months of pain.

### Tue Feb 25 — 40 minutes to 5

I used to spend 40 minutes prepping for a first call.

Full website deep dive. Annual report. Every stakeholder on LinkedIn. Custom deck. Objection map.

Very thorough. Very impressive. Very stupid.

I'd show up so loaded with research that I couldn't actually listen. I was waiting for the moment to drop my homework instead of hearing what the buyer was telling me. Like a kid in class with his hand up before the teacher finishes the question.

Now I spend 5 minutes.

Couple minutes on the website. Minute on LinkedIn (just current role and tenure. Someone 6 months in has wildly different priorities than someone 8 years in.) Minute on Google News. Minute pulling the 10-K through AI. "What are the top 3 financial priorities in this filing?" Done.

I walk into every call with a hypothesis, not a script.

"I noticed your SG&A climbing about 3% year over year. Is that on the radar or is the growth justifying the spend?"

That does more in 10 seconds than my old 40-minute prep ever did.

The less I prepare, the better my conversations get.

I know that sounds backwards. It is. That's why it works.

### Wed Feb 26 — Week 1 at Ramp, the honest version

Finished my first week at Ramp.

Monday: figured out how to get into the building.
Tuesday: learned the product enough to not sound like I was reading a brochure.
Wednesday: sat in on calls. Realized how much I don't know.
Thursday: made a comment in a deal review that showed everyone I wasn't ready to contribute yet. Cool. Cool cool cool.
Friday: stared at my notes wondering if I'd made the right decision.

That last part is the one nobody posts about. The 2 AM "what if 179% was the territory, not me?" thought. The fear that you peaked somewhere else.

Things I got wrong: I compared. A LOT. "At my last company we did it this way" came out of my mouth at least 4 times. That's poison. I know it's poison. Did it anyway.

Things I got right: I shut up more than I talked. Took notes like a maniac. Bought two people lunch and just listened to them describe their days.

Not inspirational. Not polished. That's the point.

### Thu Feb 27 — My voicemail strategy (yes I still leave voicemails)

I leave exactly 2 voicemails per prospect.

Then I stop.

This is the hill I will die on.

VM 1: 15 seconds. Name, company, one sentence. "Chad from Ramp. Saw your team is growing, wanted to talk about how finance ops scale with you. Shooting you an email now." Done.

Goal isn't a callback. Goal is name recognition. So when my email lands 30 seconds later, it's not from a complete stranger.

VM 2: few days later. 30 seconds. More specific. Shows I did homework. Quick proof point.

After that? Done. No VM 3. No VM 4. No leaving messages every week for a month like some kind of sales haunting.

Because after 2, voicemails go from "informational" to "sir please stop calling me."

My data over 5 years: 115% boost in email replies when paired with voicemails vs. email alone.

Voicemails aren't a conversion tool. They're brand awareness. That's all they do. And that's enough.

### Fri Feb 28 — There is no secret

179% average. 5 years. #1 out of 75. Platinum Club x3.

People ask me "what's the secret?" expecting some hack. Some trick. Some thing I figured out that nobody else knows.

There isn't one. I'm sorry. I wish there was. It would make a much better post.

Here's the whole thing:

First calls in 3 phases. Understanding. Alignment. Decision.

Disqualify fast. 3-5 deals at a time.

Annual audit every December. What did the wins have in common? Where did the losses go sideways?

Time management by the hour. Not the day. The hour. I know what I'm doing at 9 AM and I know what I'm doing at 3 PM.

Ego balanced with empathy. Ego to believe I belong in every room. Empathy to remember the room isn't about me.

That's it. No silver bullets. No hacks.

Just the same boring stuff, every day, for years, until it compounds into something that looks like talent but is actually just accumulated discipline.

Everyone wants the shortcut. There isn't one.

There's just the work.

(And maybe a few Blues Traveler deep dives on the drive home. But that's a different post.)
