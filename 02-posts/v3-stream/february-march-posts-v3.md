### Mon Feb 10

I keep thinking about this one moment from last quarter.

I'm on a call with a controller at a manufacturing company outside Detroit. Midsize. Maybe 400 employees. She's been in the role 11 years.

And she says something that stops me cold.

"I've known we needed to change for three years. I just couldn't get anyone to care enough to do something about it."

Three years.

She knew. Her team knew. The data was obvious. But nobody above her felt the pain enough to move.

And I realized sitting there that my job isn't to convince anyone of anything. My job is to find the people who already know and help them get the internal momentum they've been unable to build on their own.

That's a completely different thing than selling.

Selling is "let me show you why you need this."

What I actually do is "you already know you need this. Let me help you make the case to the people who control the budget."

She signed 4 months later. $187K. Not because I convinced her. She was convinced before I ever showed up.

I just gave her the language and the data to convince everyone else.

I think about her a lot. I think about how many controllers and finance directors are sitting in that exact position right now. Knowing. Waiting. Frustrated.

Three years is a long time to know something and not be able to do anything about it.

### Tue Feb 11

I got fired once.

I was 26. I'd been at the company maybe a year. I was loud. I was confident. I thought showing up and being smart was the whole job.

It wasn't.

My manager pulled me into a room on a Tuesday afternoon. There were two HR people I'd never met. One of them had a folder. That's when I knew.

I don't even remember the drive home. I remember sitting in my apartment staring at the wall thinking I had no idea what I was going to do. I had rent due in 9 days. I had $1,200 in my checking account.

The thing about getting fired is that it strips away every story you've been telling yourself. Every "I'm talented enough to figure it out" and "I'm different from everyone else" and "the rules don't apply to me."

They do apply to you. You're not different. Talent without discipline is just wasted potential with a good personality.

It took me 3 months to find another job. During those 3 months I rebuilt everything from scratch. How I prepared for calls. How I managed my time. How I followed up. How I treated the boring parts of the job that I used to skip because I thought they were beneath me.

Those 3 months built the foundation for everything that came after. The 179% average. The #1 ranking. The Platinum Club years. All of it started in that apartment staring at a wall wondering what went wrong.

I don't tell this story to be inspirational. I tell it because I think there's someone reading this who's going through something similar right now. And I want them to know that the worst moment of my career was also the most important one.

Not because everything happens for a reason. That's lazy thinking.

Because getting knocked down forced me to build something I never would have built while I was coasting.

### Wed Feb 12

Something happened on a call yesterday that I want to talk about.

I asked a buyer what happens if they don't do anything. If they just keep running things the way they're running them.

She went quiet. Like actually quiet. Not the polite "let me think about it" quiet. The kind of quiet where you can tell someone is seeing something they haven't let themselves see before.

Then she said "honestly? I think I'd lose my best analyst. She's been telling me for 6 months that she can't keep doing this manually. And I keep telling her we're working on it."

That's it. That's the whole post.

Because that moment. That specific moment where a buyer connects their inaction to a real human consequence. That's where everything changes. Not your deck. Not your pricing. Not your case study.

A person realizing that doing nothing has a cost they can feel.

I didn't say anything after she said that. I just let it sit. I let her sit with it.

Fifteen seconds of silence. Maybe twenty. Felt like five minutes.

Then she said "OK. What does the timeline look like to get this done before Q3?"

I didn't close that deal. She closed it herself. I just created the space for her to see what she already knew.

I've been selling for 15 years and I'm still amazed by how powerful silence is. How much we miss by filling every gap with another question or another slide or another value prop.

Sometimes the most important thing you can do on a call is absolutely nothing.

### Thu Feb 13

I've been thinking about something my dad said to me when I was starting out.

He's not in sales. Never has been. He's an engineer. Thinks in systems. Doesn't say much.

But when I got my first sales job he told me "the people who are best at their jobs are the ones who do the parts nobody sees."

I didn't understand it at the time. I thought sales was about the pitch. The demo. The close. The stuff people see.

It's not.

It's about the 5 minutes of research before a call that nobody will ever know about. It's about updating your CRM at 6pm on a Friday when nobody's watching. It's about sending a thoughtful follow-up that takes 20 minutes to write instead of a "just checking in" that takes 20 seconds.

It's about the discipline to do the invisible work. Day after day. For years.

My dad was right. He usually is. But I had to figure it out the hard way because I was 24 and thought I knew everything.

The gap between good reps and great reps isn't talent. It's not charisma. It's not even work ethic in the way most people think about it.

It's the willingness to do the boring, invisible, unglamorous work that compounds over time into something that looks like natural ability but is actually just accumulated discipline.

239% quota doesn't happen in the meeting room. It happens at 6pm on a Friday when you're updating your notes from the last call because you know Future You will need them.

Nobody claps for that. Nobody sees it. That's the point.

### Fri Feb 14

I want to talk about trust.

Not the LinkedIn version of trust where everyone posts "people buy from people they trust" and gets 400 likes and nothing changes.

Real trust. The kind that takes months to build and seconds to destroy.

I had a deal in 2022. Mid-market SaaS company. The VP of Finance and I had been working together for about 6 weeks. Good relationship. Honest conversations. She told me things about her org that she probably shouldn't have. That's how much she trusted me.

Then I screwed up.

I was in a meeting with her boss, the CFO, and I casually mentioned something she'd told me in confidence. I wasn't trying to be political. I wasn't trying to go around her. I just wasn't thinking.

She called me after the meeting. Her voice was flat. Not angry. Worse. Disappointed.

"I told you that because I trusted you. Not so you could use it to look smart in front of my boss."

The deal still closed. But our relationship never fully recovered. She was cordial. Professional. But the openness was gone. The real conversations stopped. I became just another vendor.

I think about that phone call probably once a month. Not because I lost a relationship, although I did. But because it taught me that trust isn't a sales technique. It's a responsibility.

When a buyer opens up to you, they're taking a risk. They're betting that you're not going to use their honesty against them. And the moment you prove them wrong, even accidentally, even with good intentions, it's over.

I never made that mistake again.

But I had to make it once to understand what trust actually costs when you break it.

### Wed Feb 19

I want to talk about the thing that happens after you leave the meeting.

Because that's where deals actually get won or lost. And almost nobody talks about it.

You crush a demo. Everyone's nodding. Your champion is fired up. They say all the right things. "This is exactly what we need." "I'm going to talk to the team." "Can you send me something I can share?"

You hang up feeling great. You update your CRM. You tell your manager the deal is moving.

Then two weeks of silence.

And you start wondering what happened. Did they lose interest? Did a competitor get in? Did the budget get pulled?

Usually it's none of those things. Usually what happened is your champion tried to sell it internally and got destroyed.

They walked into a meeting with their CFO and said "I saw this cool tool" and the CFO said "we have 14 other priorities, why should I care about this one?"

And your champion didn't have an answer. Because you never gave them one.

I lost a $340K deal this way in 2021. Everything was perfect on my side of the table. I just forgot that the real sale was happening on the other side. In rooms I'd never be in. In conversations I'd never hear.

Now I spend as much time preparing my champion for their internal meetings as I do preparing for my own. Sometimes more.

"Here's what your CFO is going to ask. Here's the data that answers it."

"Here's the objection from IT. Here's how other companies in your space handled it."

"Here's a one-page summary you can forward. Don't use my deck, it's too long. Use this."

You're not selling anymore. You're coaching. And the difference between those two things is the difference between deals that stall and deals that close.

### Thu Feb 20

Been thinking about this a lot lately.

The best year of my career. 239%, number 1 out of 75. I never once thought about my quota during a call.

Not once.

I thought about the person on the other end. What their day looks like. What's keeping them up at night. Whether I could actually help them or whether I was wasting both our time.

That's it.

The year I almost didn't make club. 2019, hit 103%. I thought about quota constantly. Every call, in the back of my head, this running calculation. "If this one closes I'm at 87%. If I pull in the deal from March I'm at 94%."

And buyers could feel it. I know they could. Because the quality of my conversations dropped. I stopped asking hard questions because I was afraid the answers would kill the deal. I stopped disqualifying because I needed every opportunity I could get. I started discounting early because I wanted the signature more than I wanted the right deal.

Quota on your mind is like commission breath for your brain. It makes you make decisions that serve you instead of the buyer. And buyers always, always know.

I don't have some profound solution for this. I don't have a trick to stop thinking about it.

I just know that the years I forgot about quota were the years I crushed it. And the years I obsessed over it were the years I barely made it.

Make of that what you will.

### Mon Feb 24

I've been having a debate with myself about honesty in sales and I want to think through it out loud.

Here's the thing. Every first call, I say some version of: "I work on commission. I've got two kids. I would love to sell you everything in our catalog. But that's not what this conversation is about."

And people always react the same way. They laugh a little. They relax. The whole energy shifts.

But lately I've been wondering. Is that honesty or is it a move? Am I being genuine or have I just learned that performing vulnerability works?

I don't have a clean answer.

I think it started genuine. The first time I said it, years ago, I said it because I was nervous and it just came out. And the buyer laughed and the call went great and I thought "huh, that worked."

And then I kept saying it. And it kept working. And now it's part of my process.

Does something stop being honest when it becomes intentional? When you know the effect it'll have?

I don't think so. Because the underlying truth hasn't changed. I do work on commission. I do have two kids. I would love to sell them everything. And I genuinely don't want to waste their time.

All of that is still true.

I think the difference is between manipulation and transparency. Manipulation is saying something false to get a reaction. Transparency is saying something true that also happens to build trust.

Both get results. Only one you can live with.

I don't know. I'm still working this one out. If you have thoughts, I'm genuinely interested.

### Tue Feb 25

Lost a deal last week.

I wasn't going to post about it because who wants to talk about losing. But that's exactly why I should.

It was a mid-market company. Software. About 200 employees. The controller and I had been talking for 6 weeks. Great conversations. She got the product, understood the ROI, had budget earmarked.

Then she emailed me on Thursday. One line. "We've decided to go in a different direction. Thanks for your time."

I sat with that email for about 20 minutes. Just staring at it.

My instinct was to respond immediately. "Can I ask why?" "Was there something we could have done differently?" "Can I get 15 minutes to understand?"

I didn't send any of that. Because I've learned that when someone tells you they've made a decision, trying to un-make it is disrespectful. They're not asking for my input. They're telling me it's over.

So I wrote back: "Completely understand. It was genuinely great working with you. If anything changes down the road, you know where to find me."

That's it.

No pitch. No last-ditch effort. No guilt trip dressed up as curiosity.

Just respect for her decision.

I found out later through a mutual connection that they went with a competitor who was 30% cheaper and had an existing relationship with their CTO.

Nothing I could have done differently. Not about my product. Not about my process. Not about me.

Sometimes you do everything right and you still lose. And the professional thing to do is accept it gracefully and move on.

I'm not good at that yet. But I'm getting better.

### Wed Feb 26

239% quota. 2023. Number 1 out of 75 reps.

You know what I think about when I think about that year?

Not the wins. I think about a healthcare deal I lost. Healthcare system in the midwest. 27 entities. 31 accountants. Complex, messy, exactly the kind of deal I'm built for.

I was 4 months into it. Deep. Multi-threaded across 6 contacts. My champion was the VP of Finance. We had a mutual plan. We had a timeline. We had internal alignment.

Or so I thought.

Turns out, the CIO's previous company had worked with a competitor. He'd known since month 1 that he wanted to go with them. My champion didn't know that. I didn't know that. We were running a process that was already decided.

The CIO never told anyone. He just waited for the evaluation to play out, then chose the company he already had a relationship with.

I found out from my champion after the decision was made. She was as blindsided as I was. Maybe more.

That deal taught me something I don't think I would have learned from winning. It taught me that the people you don't know about are often more important than the people you do. That relationships you can't see can override processes you can.

And it taught me to ask one specific question earlier: "Is there anyone in the organization who has existing relationships with vendors in this space?"

One question. I ask it in the first 2 weeks now. Every deal.

It doesn't always surface the hidden player. But when it does, it changes everything.

### Thu Feb 27

Week 2 at Ramp.

Something clicked today. Not about the product. About me.

I was shadowing a call with one of the senior reps. She was talking to a CFO at a logistics company. And I noticed something in how she asked questions.

She wasn't following a framework. She wasn't running through a discovery checklist. She was having a conversation. A real one. Where one question flowed naturally from the answer to the last one.

And I realized I used to do that. Years ago. Before I got good enough to have a "process."

Somewhere along the way, I replaced curiosity with methodology. I turned genuine interest into a repeatable system. And the system works. 179% proves it works. But watching her today reminded me of something I'd forgotten.

The system is supposed to serve the conversation. Not the other way around.

When the system becomes the point. When you're more focused on hitting your discovery questions than actually hearing the answers. You lose something. Something buyers can feel even if they can't name it.

I think that's what the transition to a new company does. It strips away the muscle memory and forces you to be present again. Because you don't have a system yet. You're just you. Trying to understand.

And "just you trying to understand" might be more powerful than any framework.

I'm going to sit with that for a while.
