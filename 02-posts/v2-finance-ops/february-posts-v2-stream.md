### Mon Feb 10 — Reimbursements shouldn't exist

I've been selling to finance teams for over a decade now and I still can't wrap my head around reimbursements.

Not the process. The concept.

We're asking employees to be the company's credit card. To float money. Sometimes for weeks. I talked to a controller last month whose average reimbursement cycle was 22 days. Twenty two days where an employee is just... out of pocket. For something they bought for the company.

And that's not even the expensive part. The expensive part is the controller spending her afternoon chasing receipts, matching GL codes, running approval workflows. All of that labor is waste. Every minute on reimbursements is a minute not on forecasting or analysis or literally anything that moves the business forward.

I keep asking the same question in discovery calls: "How many reimbursements does your team process per month?" And then: "What if that number was zero?"

The pause after that second question tells me everything. Either they've thought about it and want to talk, or they haven't thought about it and REALLY want to talk.

The best finance teams I've worked with aren't asking how to make reimbursements faster. They got to zero. Corporate cards with real-time controls. Receipts captured at the point of purchase. Approvals routed automatically before anyone has to chase anything.

The companies still doing reimbursements in 2026 aren't protecting themselves. They're protecting a process that shouldn't exist.

### Tue Feb 11 — Where the 30 days actually go

I started asking controllers to walk me through their close process. Not the theoretical version. The actual one. Hour by hour.

It's always the same story.

Five to seven days just waiting. Waiting on expense reports sitting in someone's inbox marked "I'll get to this over the weekend." Which means Tuesday. Which means the following Tuesday.

Four to five days chasing AP invoices. "Did you receive the invoice?" "Can you confirm the PO number?" "The amount doesn't match." Back and forth.

Three to four days reconciling card transactions. Matching receipts to charges. Flagging missing documentation.

Then a week of what one controller in Phoenix called "the trust but verify phase." Going back through everything because the inputs were messy and nobody trusts the outputs.

I say this with peace and love: that's not a 30 day close. That's a 7 day close buried under 23 days of broken inputs.

Talked to a company last year that went from 28 days to 6. Didn't hire anyone. Didn't work overtime. Just automated the receipt capture, put in real-time categorization, and built approval workflows that happened before month end instead of after.

22 days just... vanished.

Translation: They didn't get faster at closing. They eliminated the reasons the close was slow.

### Thu Feb 13 — The policy that nobody reads

Quick one.

A controller told me something last week I haven't stopped thinking about.

Every time there's a spending issue at her company, same thing happens. Someone in leadership says "we need a policy for that." They write a policy. Email it out. Put it in the handbook. Maybe a training.

Six months later, same issue. She showed me their expense policy. 80 pages. Beautifully written. Completely ignored.

Then she showed me a company she used to work at. Almost no written policy. Perfect compliance.

The difference? Systems. Not rules.

A policy says "all expenses over $500 require VP approval." A system makes it impossible to spend over $500 without VP approval routing automatically.

A policy says "submit receipts within 5 business days." A system captures the receipt at the point of purchase so there's nothing to submit.

When I hear "we need a policy for that" in a meeting, what I actually hear is "we don't have a system for that."

If the system existed, the policy would be unnecessary.

### Fri Feb 14 — Nobody thanks finance (Happy Valentine's Day)

I want to say something that I think needs to be said more often.

Finance teams are invisible until something goes wrong.

I've been selling to these teams for over a decade. I've sat in their offices. I've watched them work. I've seen controllers at their desks at 7 PM on the last day of the month, not because anyone asked them to, but because the work needed to get done and there wasn't enough team to do it during business hours.

They close the books. They chase receipts. They reconcile cards. They process reimbursements. They answer the same "quick questions" fifteen times a day. They support audits. They implement new tools while maintaining old ones. All with headcount that hasn't grown anywhere close to the company.

When something goes wrong? When a charge gets miscoded? When a reimbursement is late?

"Why didn't finance catch that?"

When everything runs perfectly? When the books close on time and the audit goes smooth?

Silence.

They're not building features that get announced at all-hands. They're not closing deals that get celebrated with bell rings. They're keeping the entire financial infrastructure running so everyone else can do the work that gets noticed.

Happy Valentine's Day. If there's a controller or finance director or AP specialist who makes your life easier in ways you don't even think about, tell them. They almost never hear it.

### Tue Feb 18 — 130 tools and nobody knows

Here's a number that should make every finance leader uncomfortable.

The average company uses 130 SaaS tools. One hundred and thirty.

Finance sees every single one of them. Eventually. Usually as a line item on a credit card statement that nobody recognizes.

I sat with a VP of Finance last year who pulled up their corporate card statements for Q3. She'd never had time to actually look at every line item before. We went through them together for two hours.

$47,000. Annual spend on tools nobody was using.

Not underusing. Not using.

One was a project management tool that got replaced by another project management tool 11 months earlier. Nobody cancelled the first one. The person who signed up for it had left the company.

I say this with peace and love: this isn't a finance problem. It's a company problem that finance gets stuck holding the bag for. Every department signs up for tools. Every team lead has a card. Every manager makes purchasing decisions that make sense in isolation but create chaos when you zoom out.

The difference between companies that manage spend and companies that just track it? One of them can see 130 subscriptions clearly enough to know which ones are worth keeping.

### Thu Feb 20 — Five hours a week chasing paper

I need to talk about receipts.

Not because receipts are exciting. They're the opposite of exciting. But the amount of time finance teams spend dealing with them is genuinely insane.

Five hours a week. That's what I keep hearing from controllers. Five hours chasing receipts from employees who forgot to save them, lost them, or just haven't gotten around to submitting them.

A controller in Dallas described her receipt process to me: "Every month I send the same email to the same 30 people asking for the same receipts. About 20 respond within a week. Five after a second reminder. And the last five require me to walk to their desk and stand there while they look through their phone."

She laughed when she said it. But it wasn't funny.

Five hours is not accounting. Five hours is babysitting.

And the cascade is worse than the time. Missing receipts hold up the close. Held up closes mean stale data. Stale data means decisions are being made on information that's weeks old. All because someone didn't save their lunch receipt.

Every hour a finance team spends chasing receipts is an hour not spent on analysis or forecasting or anything that actually moves the needle. Those hours add up to weeks. Those weeks add up to months.

Months of strategic capability that evaporated into a pile of missing lunch receipts.

Do you even stairmaster? Just kidding. But seriously. Ask your controller how many hours she spends on receipt chasing. The answer will ruin your day.

### Fri Feb 21 — End of week one

End of my first week at Ramp. Writing this at my kitchen table on a Friday night like a cool person.

I'm tired in a way I haven't been tired in years. Not from the work itself. From the absorbing. New product. New culture. New faces. New everything.

Things I got right this week: I listened. Really listened. Not the kind where you're already forming your response. The kind where you're just trying to understand. I asked questions that probably sounded basic. "Why does the product work this way?" First week stuff. But I'd rather ask basic questions now than pretend I understand something I don't six months from now.

Things I got wrong: I compared. A LOT. "At my last company we did it this way" came out of my mouth at least four times. That's poison. I know it's poison. Did it anyway.

Also tried to add value in a deal review on Wednesday when I didn't have enough context yet. Nobody called me out on it. But I felt it. Cool. Cool cool cool.

The temptation when you start somewhere new is to prove you belong. Especially when your whole identity is built around being good at this specific thing.

The smarter move is to prove you're coachable. Show people you're willing to be bad at something for a while so you can eventually be great at it.

That's harder than it sounds.

### Mon Feb 24 — The quick question tax

"Quick question. Can you check if this invoice was paid?"

"Quick question. What's the GL code for software subscriptions?"

"Quick question. Did we get charged for that conference registration?"

Each one takes five to ten minutes. Not because the question is hard. Because the answer requires logging into a system, pulling up a record, cross referencing a transaction, and writing a response.

I shadowed a controller for a day last quarter. She got to her desk at 8 AM planning to work on the quarterly forecast. By noon she hadn't opened the forecast file once.

Her entire morning was consumed by quick questions. Department heads. Project managers. Team leads. All needing financial information.

She wasn't doing accounting that morning. She was being a human search engine.

Fifteen of those a day. That's what she told me. Fifteen interruptions. Fifteen context switches. Fifteen detours from whatever she was actually trying to do.

And every single one of those questions could have been answered by the person asking it. "Was this invoice paid?" doesn't require specialized financial knowledge. It requires access to a system that shows payment status.

Translation: If people need to ask finance for information, the information isn't accessible enough.

---

### Tue Feb 25 — One person approves everything

Every company has this person. The one human being who approves every purchase order, every vendor payment, every subscription, every expense over a certain amount.

They become the bottleneck for the entire company. And the thing is, they don't want to be the bottleneck. They hate it.

Talked to a CFO who told me he spends 90 minutes every morning just approving things. Before he can do any of the work he was actually hired to do. Ninety minutes. Every morning.

And when he travels? Approvals stop. When he's in a back to back meeting block? Approvals stop. When he takes a vacation? Either someone scrambles to cover or things just wait.

The entire company's operational velocity is gated by one person's availability to click "approve."

One company I worked with had a 14 day average approval cycle. Not because anyone was slow. Because the process required sequential approvals from three people who were rarely all available in the same week.

They moved to tiered limits. Under $500 auto approved. Under $5,000, department head. Over $5,000, CFO. Automated routing. Parallel instead of sequential for large purchases.

Average approval time dropped to 2 days. CFO got his morning back.

I say this with peace and love: control doesn't require a single point of failure. You can have visibility AND velocity. Most companies just haven't set it up that way yet.

### Thu Feb 27 — The data is already stale

Here's the thing that keeps me up at night about finance ops.

First week of the month. Finance is in the trenches. Reconciling. Closing. Checking and double checking.

The rest of the company has already moved on. They're making decisions about this month. Setting priorities. Allocating resources. Planning spend.

And they're doing all of it without last month's data. Because the books aren't closed yet.

By the time those books are closed and the data is finalized and reviewed and distributed, it's two to three weeks old. Sometimes more.

I've seen a 3 person finance team close in 4 days. I've also seen a 15 person team take 35 days. The difference isn't headcount. It's infrastructure.

The companies that close fast don't do it by working harder during close. They make close almost unnecessary. Expense data captured in real time. AP automated. Card transactions categorized at the point of purchase. Approvals processed before month end.

By the time the first of the month arrives, 90% of the close is already done.

Translation: The close becomes a verification step instead of a construction project.

The companies doing this aren't heroes. They're not staying late. They just have systems that make the close a formality instead of a fire drill.

### Fri Feb 28 — Two weeks in

Two weeks at Ramp. End of February. Writing this one on a Sunday morning with coffee that's already cold.

The gap between "how companies have always done finance operations" and "how they could do it" is massive. I spent years selling ERP and thought I understood the problem. I understood the top layer. Below that, there are layers of manual work and workarounds and "that's just how we do it" that I never fully appreciated from the outside.

Sitting on this side of it now, I get why finance leaders are exhausted. It's not that they're not working hard enough. It's that their tools are forcing them to work on the wrong things.

I've caught myself at least a dozen times this week wanting to revert. Wanting to sell the way I've always sold. Wanting to skip the learning phase and get to the performing phase. But the whole reason I made this move was to learn something new. If I show up and just do what I've always done, I might as well have stayed.

I can give you a 10 minute explanation of what Ramp does right now. I need to get it to 30 seconds. Not for a pitch. For myself. Because if I can't explain it simply, I don't understand it well enough yet.

That's February. Not the version I'd put on a slide. The version that's true.

On to March.

(And maybe a few more cold coffees on Sunday mornings. But that's a different post.)
